A tower crane foundation is a temporary structure with one unusual load: a large overturning moment whose direction keeps changing as the crane slews, plus a storm case when the crane is out of service. "Low" cranes (mobile, crawler, self-erecting) sit on the ground through outriggers, tracks or a ballasted base, so the job is ground bearing and mats. "High" cranes (top-slewing tower cranes) need an engineered base. Above their freestanding height they are tied to, or climb inside, the building, so the building becomes part of the foundation.

Say this in the interview

The manufacturer owns the crane and gives you characteristic reactions for each case. You own the foundation: make sure the reactions match the exact configuration (mast height, jib length, counterweight), pick the governing direction (the diagonal), and check stability, ground and concrete. OSHA 1926.1435(b)(3) requires the foundation to be designed by the manufacturer or a registered professional engineer.

Characteristic, not factored

Manufacturer reactions normally include no partial factors, no dynamic factor and no second-order effect (FEM §I-2). For "high cranes" the second-order effect matters, so FEM adds an amplification factor α ≥ 1.10 on the moment when the manufacturer hasn't included it.

US layer

Since the 2012 revision, ASME B30.3 requires ASCE 7 wind for foundations and freestanding heights (ENR). Check that the site wind doesn't exceed the manufacturer's out-of-service basis, which is usually EN 14439. If it does, ask for site-specific reactions. OSHA 1926.1435(b)(4)(iii): don't exceed the manufacturer's wind limit.

Ask for the right table

Reactions depend on the mast height, mast type, jib length, counterweight, and the base or anchors. A table for a different height is a different crane. Also ask whether the values are per leg or global, whether they're at the top of the block, and whether they include second-order and dynamic effects.

Block design flow

Why the diagonal

About an axis the edge is B/2 from the center; about the diagonal the corner is B/√2 away, so overturning is safer on the diagonal but the corner pressure is higher: the kern shrinks from B/6 to 0.118B. For the same e, a square block's corner pressure on the diagonal is roughly 30–40% higher than on the axis. The tower legs see the same thing: the corner leg takes \(M/(\sqrt2D)\) on the diagonal vs. \(M/(2D)\) on the axis.

Practical details

Partial lift-off is normal under the storm case (FEM allows e up to B/3 at SLS, which is about 74% contact on the axis). Groundwater cuts the block's weight by 42%. The torsion from slewing rarely governs a block, but it goes into the anchors as shear. Leave room for backfill, the anchor template, and the mobile crane that erects the tower crane.

ACI 318-19 change that hits crane bases

Footings without stirrups now use the size-effect factor λs and the ρw1/3 term in one-way shear (Table 22.5.5.1(c)), and λs in two-way shear (Table 22.6.5.2). On a 5–6 ft crane block that can cut Vc to less than half of the old \(2\sqrt{f'_c}bd\). Thickness, not steel, usually fixes it.

Mobile crane ground support flow

Interview angle

A 20 ft timber mat is not 20 ft of bearing: only the part stiff enough to spread the load works. Check the soil (required area) and the mat (bending and shear give an effective length). Under outriggers only the timbers directly under the float carry load, so use a transition mat or a steel/composite top mat. Ask whether the track pressures are "hard ground" or "soft ground" numbers.

Outriggers

Use the manufacturer's outrigger-load software for the worst swing angle (boom over one outrigger), including empty-hook backward stability and the crane's own assembly. Then \(A=R/q_a\), check the pad or mat, and spread to any weak layer at 2:1 (Ground page sheet 6). The Lifting page, sheet 6 has the outrigger and track statics. Load charts generally assume the crane is level, typically within 1%.
